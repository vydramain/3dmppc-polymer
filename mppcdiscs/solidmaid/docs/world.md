# World, Setting & Tone

## Setting

An alternate **post-Soviet Russia, 1990s**. A grey industrial town of
Khrushchyovka apartment blocks, cracked sidewalks, courtyards, and a factory
that never really closes. Everything is worn, utilitarian, and slightly too
quiet. Beneath the mundane surface, **Alkoldun folklore** leaks in: rituals,
folk-horror spirits, and objects that behave as if they remember something.

## Tone

Satirical folk horror. Two registers held at once:

- **Mundane oppression** — the exhausting sameness of the daily grind, chronic
  fatigue, bleak weather, fluorescent light.
- **Uncanny dread** — not the world going mad, but the world going *missing*.
  The same street, the same room, the same shift, each time with one thing fewer
  in it.

The humor is deadpan and empathetic, never mocking the protagonist. The horror
is atmospheric and creeping, not gore-driven. As the closing note of the
original design put it: *make the mundane oppressive and the absurd familiar.*

## The count as fiction

The factory assembles **streetlights**. Alkoldun Vasiliusavich builds one per
shift, correctly, on time. The board over the conveyor tracks the plan, and the
plan is nearly done.

Every finished lamppost costs the town a working streetlight. Nobody arranges
this; nobody comments on it; there is no villain switching them off. It simply
holds — the quota goes down, a lamp goes out, and something is missing from the
apartment when he gets home.

> He keeps producing light, and the world keeps getting darker.

The fiction never resolves this. The board is a **bureaucratic object**, not a
supernatural one, and that is precisely what makes it frightening: the paperwork
and the vanishing are the same process seen from two sides. The horror is that
the plan is being *fulfilled*.

## The day loop as fiction

The **Home → Street → Factory → Home** cycle is the narrative spine, not just a
level structure. Each repetition is the same day with less in it. The world is
not corrupted, mutated, or rearranged — it is **worn down and subtracted from**.
Progress is measured in **what is left**, and the answer is always a smaller
number than yesterday.

This is a deliberate inversion of the earlier direction, in which the apartment
*accreted* new props and the world grew stranger each loop. Accretion reads as
escalation; subtraction reads as a countdown, and a countdown is what this game
is.

## Places

- **Home** — a single cramped Khrushchyovka apartment room. The one refuge, and
  the one that empties. A television that flips a mood flag — on the first shift
  only; after that it is gone, along with the wall it stood against. What
  remains longest is the work gear by the door. By the last shift the room is a
  place to leave, not a place to be.
- **The Street** — a short, modular stretch of town: sidewalks, apartment
  facades, fences, kiosks. Recognisable and unchanging in its geometry; what
  changes is that the lampposts stop working, one per shift, from his own
  courtyard outward. Poles stay up. Lamps go dark. The last one still burning is
  the one at the factory gate.
- **The Factory** — a compact industrial hall: an assembly table, a welding
  station, a finishing conveyor, and the board. Labour and ritual are the same
  act here, and the board is where the two meet: a work quota that is also a
  countdown.

## Motifs

- **The lamppost.** The product, the ritual object, and the unit of the count.
  Every one he finishes is one the town loses.
- **The board.** Folklore-as-bureaucracy in a single object: a production plan
  that is also a prophecy. It is the only number in the game.
- **Traces.** A pale rectangle of unfaded wallpaper, dents in linoleum, an empty
  socket, a cold pole. The game speaks in what is left behind, not in what is
  said.
- **Folklore as bureaucracy.** The bosses are folk-tale figures wearing the
  masks of 90s institutions (see [`characters.md`](characters.md)) — canon, and
  reserved for after the jam.
- **Improvised survival.** No heroic arsenal; only what's lying around — bricks,
  pipes, and the parts you build.

## Fiction guardrails

- Keep the world grounded in **authentic 1990s Russian industrial** texture; no
  modern or Western fashion, no bright folklore ornament pasted on.
- Folklore enters through **behaviour and dread**, not decoration.
- Satire stays character-empathetic; the target is the plan, not the worker.
- **Never explain the count.** No note, no voice, no character who knows. The
  board, the dark lamps, and the empty room agree with each other; that
  agreement is the entire narrative delivery.
- **Nothing is stolen or destroyed on screen.** Objects are simply not there the
  next time. No thief, no monster in the apartment, no sound of removal.
